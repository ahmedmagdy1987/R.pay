02

What R.Pay actually is

This was the most valuable thing the audit turned up, and it is not what the current homepage says. I could not verify the company externally — a web search surfaces only an unrelated “R Pay Wallet” product — so everything below is drawn from the code, chiefly the v1-original branch, which is the real business site rather than a concept.

R.Pay sells two things as one system: payment hardware bolted onto unattended machines, and the operations platform the owner uses to run them. It is not a payment gateway. The buyer is an operator who owns physical machines full of his money that he cannot see.

The customer logos in lib/assets/logos.ts are the strongest asset the company has and the site currently reduces them to a hover-pause marquee: Roshn (PIF's giga-developer), Dar Al Arkan, LuLu, Boulevard City and Boulevard World (Riyadh Season), Sela, Al Khozama, Kinan, Hamat, Malahi, Al Nadej, Al Deera, Shawarma House. The insight that changes the brief

Most of those logos are venues, not operators — developers and malls that host machines rather than own them. That means R.Pay has two audiences, and every one of the eight existing concepts addresses only one of them. Serving the venue owner is a hole in the current site, not a refinement of it.

Verified figures: 465,255 transactions · 97 machines · 9 branches · 9,434 prizes · 90/97 online · rails: mada, VISA, Mastercard, Apple Pay, stc pay, GCCNET

03

Why the previous work never produced a “wow”

The diagnosis is not that the work was bad. The engineering is genuinely top-tier — the double-scrollbar root-cause, the reduced-motion handling that never mounts a video element, the bidi discipline, LCP around 140 ms. The problem is elsewhere, and the repo's own documents already say so.

The project's research file names its own earlier hero as the cautionary example: “7-layer glow stacks — the repo's latest hero documents the hierarchy collapse.” That hero stacked an SVG network, two orbit rings, a halo, a CSS payment card with NFC ripple and sheen, a glass sales panel, two floating chips and seven-level parallax — and deleted the hero video to make room.

Three real causes

  1. Nine familiar moves, each executed at 8/10, average out to “competent”. Gradient headline, glass panel, radar sweep, marquee, sticky dashboard, ripple, count-ups, cursor glow, magnetic buttons. All well made. Not one is unfamiliar. Wow is not effects — it is proprietary form. Crop any screen, remove the logo, and nobody can name the company or even the category.
  2. Concept 08 corrected by subtraction, which was necessary but not sufficient. It went from noisy to clean. Clean is not wow. Restraint without an owned form is just quiet.
  3. Nobody was empowered to decide. Eight concepts exist because optionality replaced commitment. They even contradict each other on whether R.Pay is or serves the region's largest arcade operator.

05

The recommendation

The Bridge

برج القيادة — the ship's bridge, not a landing page

R.Pay's buyer is awake at 11pm asking four questions: is my money reaching me, is machine 43 working, has someone moved it, what did I make today. Every competitor answers with a brochure. A brochure is the wrong instrument, because the product is not information — it is command. So we do not describe the control room. We hand him the controls.

And then we turn the lights on

Halfway down, the night ends. The instruments stop, the page becomes paper in daylight, and the same company that just showed you a fleet under command shows you a printed document: who already installed it, how it compares, and what it does for the venue that hosts machines rather than owning them. Then the sun goes down and we return to the bridge to shake hands. You just experienced the transition — that is the mechanism, on this page, working.

That two-act shape does four jobs simultaneously, which is why it beat a prettier idea:

  • It is the leap — old and new are incomparable at thumbnail size.
  • It finally gives the second audience (Roshn, Boulevard, a mall's procurement lead) a room they can breathe in.
  • It solves dark-palette legibility on a cheap phone at 40% brightness in a Riyadh mall.
  • It is the surprise. Nobody who has scrolled eight sections of black instrumentation expects the page to open a window.

Why this one and not Al-Wasl

Every dependency of The Bridge is code. No photo shoot, no 3D pipeline, no typeface procurement blocking week one. Al-Wasl cannot start until a licensed or commissioned Arabic face lands — and the type is the longest-lead item in the project. The Bridge ships without it and gets better with it. That is the difference between a plan and a coin flip.

Both processes independently converged on the same conclusions — kill the hub, delete the dead video, no gradients, no fake dashboards, real artifacts with timestamps. Where they converged, I have high confidence. Where they diverged, I have taken The Bridge's architecture and grafted Al-Wasl's best object into it: the receipt — وصل, which in Arabic means both the join and the receipt. It lives on paper, in Act II, and it needs no disclaimer because it is true.

The one law that carries everything

Cyan means live. Never a border, never a button, never a heading, never decoration. If something is cyan, it is reporting real state — twelve consuming nodes site-wide, lint-enforced. Rationing is what makes it read as competence instead of ornament. In Act II there is no cyan at all, because in daylight nothing needs a status light.

Typography

Body and UI: Readex Pro 400/600 — already on disk. Telemetry, numerals and log lines: IBM Plex Mono, also on disk, tabular figures. Mono reads as machine-generated, therefore true. For display, two tracks: ship on Noto Kufi Arabic 700 day one, upgrade to a licensed TPTQ Greta Sans Arabic or 29LT Zarid Sans when you decide. Total font budget ~112 kB, two preloaded.

Two rules that are not negotiable. :lang(ar) { letter-spacing: 0 !important } — Latin tracking severs cursive joining and instantly marks a site as foreign-made. And no Arabic is ever revealed character by character; typing effects break cursive joining mid-word, so every “typing” is a mask sweep over an already-shaped line. Three of the six pitches got that wrong.

Motion, scroll and the performance contract

No animation library. Native CSS scroll-driven animations where supported, one shared rAF broker as the fallback, Web Animations API for the hero stagger, custom properties for all interpolable state. Five client islands, ~14 kB gzipped total. The frame scrub uses createImageBitmap into a canvas ring buffer — never <video> seeking, which stalls 200–600 ms on mid-tier Android.

Rejected outright: GSAP, Framer Motion, Lenis, Tailwind, all WebGL and 3D, three@0.149.0 (unused, ~600 kB), scroll hijacking, backdrop-filter and animated blur.

CI-enforced budget — fails the PR, not production

First Load JS ≤ 110 kB · LCP ≤ 1.8s on Moto G Power / Fast 3G · CLS ≤ 0.02 · INP < 200ms · Lighthouse a11y = 100 · mobile first view ≤ 550 kB

And the single best test in the whole plan, borrowed from the Daylight concept: “remove all motion and it is still 95% as good.” Playwright is already a devDependency — it renders every page with prefers-reduced-motion forced and diffs it against the animated build. That one sentence is simultaneously a motion spec, an accessibility spec and a performance budget.

Mobile

Designed on its own terms, not scaled down. The rail docks to the bottom edge; the tick index collapses to a 3px hairline; the radar is better on touch — dragging a machine out of its zone with your thumb is more visceral than with a cursor, with a haptic tick at the boundary crossing. Act II exists partly because of mobile, since a dark page at 40% brightness in a mall concourse is hard to read.

The three signature moments

  1. اسرق المكينة — Steal the machine. Drag the machine outside its geofence. The ring snaps red, the render desaturates, and the day's revenue counter freezes mid-digit. Release it and the counter resumes from where it stopped, not from zero — which converts “my machine died” into “my money was preserved”. That is the actual sale, for the cost of one variable. Fifth attempt earns the only joke on the site.
  2. الريال الذي عاد — The riyal that came back. Its own section, its own silence. A payment fails and the money travels backwards on its own. No competitor can show this.
  3. مطلع النهار — Daybreak. The act break itself. The cheapest and least copyable moment in the plan, because it is structural rather than technical.

Note what is deliberately not here: no hero video, no 3D, no WebGL, no particles, no gradient. Every one of those was available and every one was rejected on evidence.

07

Tools — what helps, what doesn't

Figma — yes, but sequenced, and it needs an upgrade

Figma should not lead. There is no Figma library in this project; every decision to date was made in CSS and verified by screenshot. Making Figma the source of truth for a compositor-timed animation wastes weeks and produces a document that is wrong the day it is signed. The correct sequence:

  1. Code is the source of truth. Tokens live in the repo, pushed one-way into Figma Variables.
  2. Generate the library from the tokens — about nine components. Every one needs an AR and an EN variant, bound to dir: rtl | ltr variable modes, because Arabic runs 20–25% shorter and takes different leading. A library built only in Latin produces designs that break in the primary language.
  3. Code Connect last, so Dev Mode shows real JSX. ~20 min per component thereafter — the cheapest governance available.

Figma holds the storyboard and the composed stills. It does not hold the radar, the scrub or the act break.

Blocker

Your Figma account is a View seat on a Starter team. That cannot edit files, cannot publish libraries, and cannot use multi-mode variables — all three of which the plan above needs. You need Figma Professional with a Full/Edit seat (Dev Mode for Code Connect comes with it). Until then I can read from Figma but not author in it.

Higgsfield — yes, but narrowly

You are on Ultra with 1,774 credits, and the repo shows this pipeline already worked (~430 credits, all image-to-image against canonical renders). But the manifest is also a confession: rejected generations included a reader label corrupted to “B.PAY”, invented storefront lettering reading “CUCCI”, and garbled Arabic screen text that shipped in v1 and had to be regenerated.

So: no AI hero film. The one honest use is 320×180 sensor-feed loops — inside a surveillance frame with a timecode, generated footage reads as a camera feed and its artefacts are invisible at that size. Zero to three clips, ≤250 kB each. Never full-bleed, never above the fold.

08

The risk that would kill this

All three judging panels named the same one independently, so I am giving it its own section rather than burying it in a list.

R1 — If the data feels fake, the concept inverts

This direction stakes everything on the visitor believing the telemetry is real. The instant someone senses that “90 of 97 online” is decorative, the site stops reading as competence and starts reading as theatre — and a payments company caught performing competence it doesn't have is worse off than one that shipped a nice gradient.

The mitigation is non-negotiable. Every figure carries a visible provenance chip with a date. The radar is explicitly labelled محاكاة تفاعلية · لا يتم إيقاف أي جهاز حقيقي. Every counter takes { value, asOf, isLive } from day one, so going live later is a data swap rather than a rewrite. If R.Pay will not commit to either a live aggregate endpoint or explicitly dated static figures, do not build this concept — pick Daylight instead, which needs no live data at all.

Two more worth naming now. You may lose your nerve on Act II — a white section screenshots as “unfinished” out of context, and someone will ask أين التصميم؟. So we build it second, in week two, and put the Daybreak scroll on your phone before section 03 exists; you must feel the act break, not evaluate a static frame. And naming SurePay and Geidea in the comparison is comparative advertising against two larger licensed competitors — every row needs a source, a date and legal sign-off, with a no-names fallback kept one commit away.
